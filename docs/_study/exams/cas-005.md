## Exam format

As of 2026-10-24, the [SecurityX certification page](https://www.comptia.org/en-us/certifications/securityx/)
and the objectives document (version 3.0) state:

| | |
|---|---|
| Exam code | CAS-005 (SecurityX V5; the CASP+ name was retired with this version) |
| Launched | December 17, 2024 |
| Questions | Maximum of 90, a mix of multiple-choice and performance-based questions |
| Length | 165 minutes |
| Passing score | Pass/fail only; there is no scaled score |
| Languages | English and Japanese; others to be determined |
| Recommended experience | At least 10 years of hands-on IT experience, including 5 years of hands-on security |
| Retirement | Usually three years after launch; CompTIA estimates 2027 |
| Renewal | Valid three years, renewed through the CompTIA Continuing Education program (75 CEUs for SecurityX, or by passing a qualifying higher-level exam) |

The objectives document also says the bulleted examples under each
objective are **not exhaustive**: the exam can test related technologies that
the document never names. Confirm the price, languages and retirement date on
the official page before booking, because all three change without notice.

The objectives above are the 23 numbered objectives from the official
document, transcribed at the objective level. Each objective carries two or
three levels of bulleted example topics in the PDF; the library maps its
articles to the objective, not to individual bullets.

## What the exam really tests

SecurityX sits at the top of CompTIA's cybersecurity pathway and is explicitly
a **practitioner-architect** exam: it does not test whether you can define
zero trust, it tests whether you can choose, integrate and troubleshoot the
controls that make zero trust real in a messy enterprise. Nearly every
objective starts with "Given a scenario", and the performance-based questions
put you in front of configuration fragments, logs, packet captures and
architecture diagrams and ask what is wrong or what to change.

The four domains weight the exam towards building and running things:

- **Security Engineering (31%)** is the largest domain and the most hands-on:
  IAM troubleshooting (federation, certificate and token problems, conditional
  access failures), endpoint and server hardening, network security
  troubleshooting across segmentation, VPN, DNS and PKI issues, hardware
  security (TPM, HSM, secure boot, attestation), specialized and legacy
  systems (OT/ICS, embedded, medical, vehicle systems), automation and
  scripting for security, and both the theory and the use cases of modern
  cryptography (post-quantum cryptography, forward secrecy, homomorphic
  encryption, key stretching, tokenization, cryptographic erase).
- **Security Architecture (27%)** covers resilient design (availability,
  scaling, failure modes), security across the systems life cycle, the design
  of access and authorization systems, secure cloud adoption (CASB, shared
  responsibility, CI/CD pipeline security, data remanence, key management) and
  zero trust (deperimeterization, SASE, SD-WAN, subject-object relationships).
- **Security Operations (22%)** covers monitoring and data analysis (SIEM
  tuning, false positives and negatives, aggregate analysis), vulnerabilities
  and attack surface, threat hunting and intelligence (OSINT, ISACs, STIX and
  TAXII, threat intelligence platforms), and incident response analysis
  (malware analysis, sandboxing, indicator extraction, reverse engineering,
  metadata and volatile-memory analysis).
- **Governance, Risk, and Compliance (20%)** covers security program
  documentation and management, governance frameworks (COBIT, ITIL),
  configuration management, GRC tooling, risk management (quantitative versus
  qualitative, third-party and supply-chain risk, availability, confidentiality,
  integrity and privacy risk), compliance strategies and frameworks, threat
  modeling (attacker characteristics, attack patterns, STRIDE, ATT&CK, CAPEC)
  and, new in this version, the security challenges of AI adoption (prompt
  injection, insecure output handling, excessive agency, model theft, data
  poisoning, and legal and privacy questions).

Where candidates lose points: treating it as a bigger Security+, memorising
definitions instead of practising decisions, weak hands-on troubleshooting of
IAM and PKI, and underestimating the cryptography objectives, which expect you
to reason about *why* a technique fits a use case rather than to recite
algorithm names.

## Study strategy

Study in the order the library's security track is built, because SecurityX
assumes everything beneath it:

1. **Foundations and identity first.** Work through
   [security foundations](../../security/index.md) and the identity and access
   pages until OAuth 2.0, OpenID Connect, SAML, certificate-based
   authentication and conditional access are second nature. Objective 3.1
   (IAM troubleshooting) is where a .NET developer's background with
   Microsoft Entra ID becomes an advantage: you have debugged token audiences,
   scopes and clock skew already, so practise the same failures with SAML
   federation and certificate chains.
2. **Architecture and zero trust.** Use the cloud track's identity, networking
   and governance pages for both Azure and AWS. SecurityX is vendor-neutral,
   but every cloud objective (2.5, 2.6) is easier when you have built private
   endpoints, CASB-style controls and microsegmentation on a real platform.
3. **Engineering depth.** Hardware security, specialized systems and advanced
   cryptography are the areas most readers have not touched. Study the
   cryptography pages with the objectives open beside you, and build a small
   lab: TPM attestation on a VM, a private CA with certificate rotation, and a
   post-quantum-capable TLS 1.3 configuration.
4. **Operations.** Practise reading SIEM output, writing detections, and
   working through a malware triage with a sandbox. The detection and response
   pages and the incident tabletops in [Practice](../../practice/index.md)
   cover this ground.
5. **GRC and AI.** Read the governance, risk and compliance pages last; they are
   mostly vocabulary and judgement. Pay attention to objective 1.5, which is
   new: the OWASP Top 10 for LLM applications and the agentic-applications list
   are the best primary sources.

CompTIA's own resources are the CertMaster Learn, Labs and Practice products
and the official study guide, all linked from the certification page. The
objectives PDF is the single most useful document: use it as a checklist and
make sure you can say something concrete about every bullet.

What transfers from your existing preparation: CKAD and container work maps
to the automation, CI/CD and cloud objectives; the Azure security pages in
this library map to 2.5 and 3.1; the threat-modeling material maps directly
to 1.4. What misleads: Azure-specific product names are never the answer on
a vendor-neutral exam, so learn the generic term for every Microsoft feature
you know (conditional access is "context-aware access control", Defender for
Cloud is "CSPM/CWPP", Sentinel is "SIEM/SOAR").

## Differences from related exams

- **CAS-005 vs SC-100 (Microsoft Cybersecurity Architect).** Both are
  architect-level, but SC-100 is a design exam about the Microsoft stack and
  its reference architectures, with no hands-on troubleshooting. SecurityX is
  vendor-neutral and hands-on, with performance-based questions and a heavier
  engineering and cryptography load. SC-100 has prerequisites; SecurityX has
  none.
- **CAS-005 vs CISSP.** CISSP is broader and more managerial: eight domains,
  a five-year experience requirement, and questions written from the point of
  view of a security manager choosing the best option. SecurityX is deeper on
  implementation and troubleshooting and has no experience requirement,
  although CompTIA recommends ten years. Many people hold both: SecurityX for
  the hands-on credibility and CISSP for the management track.
- **CAS-005 vs ISSAP.** ISSAP is the CISSP concentration for security
  architecture and requires a CISSP plus two years of architecture experience.
  It is pure design; SecurityX pairs design with engineering and operations.
- **CAS-005 vs Security+ (SY0-801), CySA+ (CS0-004) and SecAI+ (CY0-001).**
  Security+ is the entry exam, CySA+ is the analyst exam and SecAI+ is the
  AI-security exam. SecurityX assumes all three and asks you to integrate
  them. Its AI coverage (objective 1.5) is one objective out of 23, where
  SecAI+ treats AI as the whole exam.

## Exam-day notes

- Performance-based questions appear early and take longest. Flag any that
  stall you and return after the multiple-choice questions; at 165 minutes
  for up to 90 items you have under two minutes per question on average.
- Read the scenario for the constraint that decides the answer. SecurityX
  questions usually contain one detail (a compliance requirement, a legacy
  system that cannot be patched, an availability target) that eliminates the
  otherwise-reasonable options.
- Prefer the answer that addresses root cause over the one that adds another
  control. Troubleshooting objectives reward diagnosis.
- Know the cryptography trade-offs cold: performance versus security, forward
  secrecy versus session resumption, key stretching versus hardware
  acceleration. These are quick points if you have practised them and slow
  guesses if you have not.
- There is no scaled score and no published cut-off, so do not try to
  estimate your result during the exam. Answer every question; unanswered
  items count as wrong.
