## About this certification

The **iSAQB** (International Software Architecture Qualification Board, a non-profit association) runs the **CPSA** scheme: Foundation Level (CPSA-F), Advanced Level (CPSA-A) and Expert Level. Independent licensed certification bodies hold the exams, so prices vary by country and provider. CPSA-F is iSAQB's own name for the certification. It has no separate exam number.

### How the objectives above were built

The curriculum is the outline. Version **2025.1-rev2-EN-20250427**, valid from April 1, 2025 ([PDF](https://public.isaqb.org/curriculum-foundation/curriculum-foundation-en.pdf), [HTML](https://public.isaqb.org/curriculum-foundation/curriculum-foundation-en.html), maintained in the public [GitHub repository](https://github.com/isaqb-org/curriculum-foundation)) has six chapters and 38 learning goals (LGs). Each chapter is an objective here, and each LG is a second-level objective with its curriculum number and **relevance tag**:

- **R1 – "being able to":** you can apply it independently. *Will be* part of the exam.
- **R2 – "understanding":** you understand it in principle. *May be* part of the exam.
- **R3 – "knowing":** background knowledge. *Will not be* examined.

Many LGs mix tags (for example "R1-R3"), because some sub-items inside the LG are R1 and others are R3. The curriculum marks each sub-item, so read the LG text for the detail. The R tags matter more than anything else in planning your study time. iSAQB publishes no per-chapter exam weights. It gives only recommended *teaching* times: chapter 1 120 min, chapter 2 180, chapter 3 360, chapter 4 240, chapter 5 90 and chapter 6 90, a total of 1,080 minutes. Design and Development is clearly the biggest chapter. The CPSA-F page says each curriculum release cycle is two years, so check for a newer version before you book. LG 04-04 "Learning Goal not Found" is real: the curriculum says the title was chosen on purpose and the goal is that architects can deal gracefully with unexpected situations.

## Exam format

Facts are as of 2026-10-24, from the [Foundation Level exam page](https://www.isaqb.org/certifications/cpsa-exams/foundation-level-exam/) and the [Examination Rules](https://public.isaqb.org/examination-foundation/examination_rules/examination-rules-en.pdf) (version 2020.2-EN). Confirm them with your certification body before booking.

- **Question types:** multiple choice in three styles. **A** (single correct answer). **P** (pick a stated number of correct answers from a list, with partial credit and a deduction for each wrong tick). **K** (allocate each statement to one of two categories, such as "applicable / not applicable", with points per correct allocation and deductions for wrong ones). A question's score never goes below 0, and penalties never spill into other questions.
- **Number of questions:** about 40, drawn at random and worth 1–2 points each, so the exact count varies.
- **Duration:** 75 minutes. 90 minutes for non-native speakers (and for profoundly visually impaired candidates), if you request it at registration.
- **Passing score:** 60% of the maximum possible points.
- **Book policy:** closed book. No aids are allowed.
- **Price:** set by each certification body and country. iSAQB lists no single fee. Accredited trainings often include the exam.
- **Languages:** English, German, Italian and Spanish.
- **Delivery:** remote online exam from home; paper or tablet exams after accredited classroom training; public exams from certification bodies; Pearson VUE test centres in more than 190 countries.
- **Results:** within one to two weeks (the FAQ says usually within a week).
- **Retakes:** allowed with no waiting period.
- **Validity:** **for life**. CPSA certification does not expire and has no renewal.
- **Practice:** an official [mock exam](https://public.isaqb.org/foundation-exam-questions/mock-en.pdf) with 38 questions based on the real exam, with solutions.

## What the exam really tests

CPSA-F tests whether you think like the architect of one software system:

- **Definitions and role.** What counts as architecture (building blocks, interfaces, relationships, cross-cutting concepts and decisions). What an architect does, from clarifying requirements to accompanying implementation. How the role relates to product owners, project managers, developers, testers, operations and enterprise architects.
- **Requirements and quality.** Stakeholder concerns, the many kinds of constraints (technical, organisational including Conway's law, regulatory, and trends), the ISO 25010 and Q42 quality models, trade-offs between qualities, and **quality scenarios** (context, stimulus, response, measurement). Expect questions on which statements are valid quality scenarios, or which constraints are organisational.
- **Design.** The largest chapter: top-down and bottom-up approaches, iterative design, domain-driven design heuristics, design principles (abstraction, modularity, coupling and cohesion, information hiding, SOLID), dependency management (including dependency injection), interface design, architectural patterns (layers, pipes and filters, microservices, ports and adapters and others), and cross-cutting concerns.
- **Communication.** Documentation requirements, views (context, building block, runtime and deployment), notations such as UML, documenting interfaces and decisions (ADRs), and templates such as arc42.
- **Analysis.** Why to evaluate, qualitative, scenario-based analysis vs. quantitative metrics, and checking that the code conforms to the decisions.

**Where candidates lose points:** P and K questions punish over-ticking, so a half-confident extra tick can wipe out the points you earned. Many candidates also spend study time on R3 material, which is never examined, and get caught by iSAQB's precise use of terms such as "building block" vs. "component" and "cross-cutting concern".

## Study strategy

1. **Read the curriculum twice:** first for structure, then marking every R1 and R2 item. Make the R1 items your flashcards.
2. **Learn the vocabulary iSAQB uses.** The curriculum references the arc42 template and the Q42 quality model throughout. Both come from the same community and use the same terms.
3. **Practise quality scenarios.** Write ten scenarios for a system you know, such as an ASP.NET Core API with an Angular front end. The curriculum (LG 02-04) asks for context, stimulus, response and measurement in each one.
4. **Document a real system.** Draw a context view, a building block view (two levels), one runtime scenario and a deployment view, and write two decision records. This one exercise covers most of chapter 4.
5. **Take the mock exam** under time pressure (75 minutes) and score it with the official scoring rules, including deductions, to calibrate how many P and K ticks are worth risking.
6. **Accredited training is optional.** If you want it, iSAQB lists accredited providers and certification bodies on its site. A three-day course is the minimum the curriculum expects.

**How your background helps or misleads**

- *.NET/Angular experience* is a strong base. Layering, dependency injection, interfaces, SPA and backend separation and REST design are all in chapter 3. Be careful: iSAQB uses neutral terms. "Dependency injection" here is a design principle, not the ASP.NET Core service container.
- *AZ-204 and CKAD preparation* help with LG 03-11 (deployment) and LG 03-12 (distributed systems), but both are **R3** and never examined. Do not overinvest there.
- *AZ-900 and cloud thinking* can mislead. CPSA-F never asks about cloud services. "Scalability" is a quality to make concrete with a scenario, not an auto-scale setting.

## Differences from related exams

- **CPSA-F vs OGEA-101 (TOGAF EA Foundation).** These are different layers of architecture. CPSA-F covers the internal structure and qualities of *one system*. TOGAF covers *enterprise-wide* architecture and its method (ADM), governance and landscape. The CPSA-F curriculum marks enterprise IT architecture as outside its focus (LG 01-05, R3). TOGAF Part 1 is 40 questions in 60 minutes, one point each, no penalties. CPSA-F is about 40 questions in 75 minutes, with partial credit and deductions on P and K questions.
- **CPSA-F vs CPSA-A.** CPSA-F is a knowledge exam. CPSA-A needs CPSA-F, 70 credit points of advanced module training and three years of experience, then a roughly 40-hour written design assignment and an oral defence. Think of CPSA-F as "knows the craft" and CPSA-A as "has shown the craft on a realistic system".
- **CPSA-F vs AZ-305 and SAA-C03.** Cloud architect exams ask which managed service meets a requirement. CPSA-F asks how to structure, decide on and document a system so it meets its quality goals, whatever the platform. Learning CPSA-F first gives you language (quality scenarios, views and ADRs) for justifying AZ-305 and SAA-C03 choices.
- **CPSA-F vs Claude Certified Architect – Foundations.** The Claude exam tests one vendor's agent stack (Agent SDK, Claude Code, MCP). CPSA-F tests vendor-neutral design craft. The AGENTA and SWARC4AI Advanced modules connect the two.

## Exam-day notes

- **Budget:** 75 minutes for about 40 questions is under 2 minutes each. Do the A questions fast and give the remaining time to P and K questions.
- **Read the header.** Each question shows its points and type. For P questions, the text says how many answers to pick, and picking more scores 0.
- **Tick only when sure.** On P and K questions each wrong tick costs as much as a right one earns. Leaving a single uncertain line blank is often better than guessing.
- **Apply R1 reasoning, not trivia.** If an option relies on a fact you only recognise from R3 material, it is unlikely to be the deciding point.
- **Watch absolutes.** Statements with "always" or "never" about design principles (for example "loose coupling is always better") are usually false in iSAQB's trade-off-oriented view.
