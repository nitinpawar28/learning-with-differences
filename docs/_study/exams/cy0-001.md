## What the exam really tests

SecAI+ is a vendor-neutral exam about AI and security in both directions: securing AI systems, and using AI for security work. Its four domains are Basic AI Concepts Related to Cybersecurity (17%), Securing AI Systems (40%), AI-assisted Security (24%) and AI Governance, Risk, and Compliance (19%).

**Domain 2 is the heart of the exam.** Its six objectives, every one of them *Given a scenario* or *Given a set of requirements*, cover:

- AI threat-modeling resources: the OWASP Top 10 for LLMs, the OWASP Machine Learning Security Top 10, MITRE ATLAS, the MIT AI Risk Repository and the CVE AI Working Group
- model controls and gateway controls: guardrails, prompt templates, prompt firewalls, rate limits, token limits and input quotas
- access control for models, data, agents and APIs
- data security for AI: encryption in transit, at rest and in use, plus anonymization, redaction, masking and classification labels
- monitoring and auditing: prompt and response monitoring, log sanitization and protection, response confidence, rate monitoring and AI cost monitoring
- reading the evidence of an attack (backdoors, trojans, prompt injection, poisoning and more) and choosing compensating controls

**Domain 1 is the vocabulary.** It covers the types of AI (generative AI, machine learning, transformers, deep learning) and training techniques (supervised, unsupervised, reinforcement learning). It also covers prompt engineering (system prompts and user prompts, one-shot and multi-shot prompting), data lineage and provenance, watermarking, RAG with vector stores and embeddings, the security of each stage of the AI life cycle, and human-in-the-loop design.

**Domain 3 covers AI as a tool for defenders and for attackers.** For defenders: IDE, browser and CLI plug-ins, AI agents, AI in CI/CD (code scanning, software composition analysis), and AI-assisted change management and ticket triage. For attackers: deepfakes, AI-driven reconnaissance and social engineering, obfuscation, and automated generation of attacks, payloads and malware.

**Domain 4 is governance.** It covers an AI Center of Excellence and the AI-related roles, responsible-AI principles, shadow AI, the EU AI Act, OECD standards, ISO AI standards, the NIST AI RMF, sanctioned versus unsanctioned models, and data sovereignty.

**Where candidates lose points:**

- **Mixing up attack classes.** Prompt injection, jailbreaking, data poisoning, model inversion, membership inference and model theft each need a different control. The exam gives you the evidence (a log, a prompt and its response, a spike in token use) and you must name the attack *and* the right compensating control.
- **Gateway control vs model control.** A prompt firewall or a rate limit sits in front of the model. A guardrail or a system prompt is part of the model's configuration. Questions often hinge on where a control belongs.
- **Cost and abuse.** AI cost monitoring and token quotas are listed as security controls, which surprises many security people.
- **Framework recall.** You need to know what each framework is *for*: ATLAS catalogues adversary tactics against AI, the OWASP LLM Top 10 lists application risks, the NIST AI RMF is a risk-management framework, and the EU AI Act is regulation with risk tiers.

## Study strategy

**Order of study:**

1. **Domain 1, the concepts.** You cannot reason about poisoning without knowing what training is, or about RAG leakage without knowing what an embedding is.
2. **Domain 2 threat models.** Read the official OWASP Top 10 for LLM Applications and browse MITRE ATLAS. Map each risk to the controls in objectives 2.2–2.5.
3. **The Domain 2 controls, in a lab** (see below).
4. **Domain 3.** This goes quickly if you already use AI coding assistants.
5. **Domain 4, the governance names.** Know the purpose and scope of the EU AI Act, the NIST AI RMF, the ISO AI standards (ISO/IEC 42001 is the management-system standard) and the OECD AI principles.

**Hands-on work that pays off:**

- Build a small RAG app (in Microsoft Foundry, Azure OpenAI or Amazon Bedrock, or locally) and try indirect prompt injection through a poisoned document. Then add content filters or a guardrail and watch them block it.
- Put an API gateway (Azure API Management or Amazon API Gateway) in front of a model endpoint and configure token or rate limits. This is objective 2.2's "gateway controls" made concrete.
- Turn on prompt and response logging, then practise sanitizing secrets and PII out of those logs (2.5).
- Give an agent a tool with deliberately excessive permissions and observe what goes wrong. This is objective 2.3's "agent access".

**Official learning resources.** The [SecAI+ page](https://www.comptia.org/en-us/certifications/secai/) lists CertMaster Perform (lessons, video, simulated and live virtual-machine labs, assessments and practice tests) and CertMaster Labs (a live virtual lab environment with guided tasks), and links to bundles in the CompTIA Store. The free [objectives PDF](https://lecbyo.files.cmp.optimizely.com/download/856bb680aa9f11f09c4adeeca108c729) lists every bullet and an acronym list. Work from it.

**How your background helps or misleads:**

- **AZ-204 prep and .NET development** give you a working knowledge of APIs, API Management, Key Vault and managed identities, which is the plumbing that AI gateway and access controls run on. Developers do well on objectives 3.1 and 3.3 (AI in IDEs and CI/CD).
- **Angular front-end work** helps you picture insecure output handling: a model's output rendered as HTML is untrusted input, just like user input.
- **CKAD prep** helps with the deployment and runtime parts of the AI life cycle (containers, secrets, network policies around model servers).
- **The misleading part.** This exam is vendor-neutral. Product names you know from Azure AI Content Safety or Bedrock Guardrails will not appear. Learn the generic name for each capability, such as prompt firewall, guardrail or output filter.
- **AZ-900** gives you only the cloud basics. The AI content here goes well beyond the AI workloads overview in AZ-900.

## Differences from related exams

- **CY0-001 vs SY0-801 (Security+ V8).** Security+ V8 has one AI objective (2.6), which names the threats. SecAI+ spends 40% of the exam on the controls against those threats, plus AI-assisted operations and AI governance. SecAI+ is about 60 questions in 60 minutes; Security+ is 90 in 90.
- **CY0-001 vs AAISM (ISACA Advanced in AI Security Management).** AAISM is for security *managers* who already hold CISM or CISSP. It tests governance, program management and risk decisions (90 multiple-choice questions, 150 minutes, no labs). SecAI+ is practitioner-level and includes PBQs where you choose and apply controls. SecAI+ is about *how* to implement controls, AAISM is about *whether* and *who*.
- **CY0-001 vs OSAI (OffSec AI Red Teamer).** OSAI is a 24-hour hands-on attack exam against agents, RAG pipelines, MCP tool surfaces and AI infrastructure. SecAI+ asks you to recognize those attacks and choose defences. Taking SecAI+ first gives you the vocabulary for OSAI.
- **CY0-001 vs AIGP (IAPP).** AIGP is about AI governance and law: policy, privacy and the regulation life cycle. SecAI+ touches on that in Domain 4 only, at 19%.
- **CY0-001 vs Microsoft SC-500 and AI-901.** SC-500's "Implement security for AI" skill group covers the same ideas through specific products (Defender for AI services, Purview DSPM, the AI gateway in API Management). SecAI+ is the vendor-neutral theory behind them. AI-901 is AI fundamentals with no security focus.
- **CY0-001 vs CAS-005 (SecurityX).** SecurityX objective 1.5 covers AI adoption risks (prompt injection, insecure output handling, excessive agency) as one of 23 objectives. SecAI+ treats AI as the whole exam.

## Exam-day notes

- **Budget one minute per question,** with no slack: up to 60 questions in 60 minutes. Skip a PBQ at first if it looks long, answer the multiple-choice questions, and come back to it.
- **Name the stage first.** In scenario questions, work out where in the AI life cycle the problem sits (data collection, training, deployment or monitoring). The right control usually belongs to that stage.
- **Decide what "the attacker controls" means.** In injection questions, ask whether the attacker controls the user prompt (direct injection) or the retrieved content (indirect injection). That decides whether the fix is input filtering, retrieval-source trust or output handling.
- **Read for least privilege.** Many agent and tool scenarios are solved by restricting what the agent can call or read, not by a smarter prompt.
- **There is no penalty for guessing.** Answer everything.
